- [[a-rejoinder-to-king]] — A rejoinder to King — Freedman, Klein, Ostland & Roberts's rejoinder (2 March 1999) to King's JASA reply, extending the empirical debate with new tables for non-Hispanics (Stockton, LA) and men/women (South Carolina block groups, obtained directly from the Census Bureau). Table 1 shows King's method deviates from truth by up to 33 SEs on party affiliation among non-Hispanics in LA and 24 SEs on education; the neighborhood model wins in nearly every cell of Table 2's method-comparison matrix — King's method 'does not appear in the table because in each case it does less well than the neighborhood model.' On King's own South Carolina poverty data (his book's own difficult test case), the neighborhood model beats King's method for both men and women. Documents a specific software bug: feeding EZIDOS-generated artificial data back into EZIDOS produces an E{t|x} diagnostic plot where 'about half' of simulated data points spill outside the model's own 80% confidence band — a diagnostic failure on data generated exactly according to the model's assumptions. Directly rebuts King's charge of non-cooperation: states Freedman et al. had earlier asked King for his data and were refused, told instead to use files requiring 'an HP workstation running UNIX and GAUSS' yielding only 'a long string of unidentified numbers' — proposes a mutual data-release pact (never apparently consummated). Cites two additional independent EI critiques: Stoto (1998, Public Health Reports) and Tam (1998, Political Analysis 'Iff the assumption fits'), plus McCue (1998, Caltech technical report 'Deconstructing King'). Concludes: 'inferring the behavior of subgroups from aggregate data is generally impossible: the relevant parameters are not identifiable' — a claim the authors say King himself effectively concedes. `evergreen`
- [[ecological-inference-and-the-ecological-fallacy]] — Ecological Inference and the Ecological Fallacy — Freedman's 1999 encyclopedia entry (Technical Report 549) is the canonical pedagogical statement of the ecological fallacy and a critique of ecological-inference (EI) methods including King's (1997) random-coefficients model. Uses Robinson's (1950) 1930 US Census example: across the 48 states, the ecological correlation between percent foreign-born and percent literate is +.53, but the individual-level correlation is -.11 — the ecological correlation gives the wrong sign. Replicates the reversal with 1995 Current Population Survey data on nativity and income: ecological correlation +.52 (suggesting foreign-born have higher incomes), but truth is 35% of native-born vs 28% of foreign-born have high income (individual correlation -.05) — driven by immigrants concentrating in richer states (CA, HI, NY vs KY, TN, WV). Presents the 'method of bounds' (Duncan & Davis 1953) using Washington state 1995 data: with only marginals known (7.9% foreign-born, 34.4% high-income), the native-born high-income share is bounded between 28.8% and 37.4% — often too wide to be useful. Table 2 directly compares neighborhood model (34%/36%), ecological regression (29%/85%), and King's random-coefficients EZIDOS model (30%/72%) against ground truth (35%/28%) on the nativity-income data: the neighborhood model is dramatically closer to truth than either regression-based method, which get the foreign-born estimate off by roughly 3x (85% and 72% vs true 28%). Concludes aggregation bias and confounding 'are unlikely to be resolved in the proximate future.' `evergreen`
- [[on-solutions-to-the-ecological-inference-problem]] — On “Solutions” to the Ecological Inference Problem — Freedman, Klein, Ostland & Roberts (2450 UC Berkeley/RAND) empirically test Gary King's EI method (EZIDOS software v1.31) against ecological regression and the neighborhood model on three benchmark datasets where ground truth is known: the 1988 Stockton exit poll (39 precincts), 1980 census/1988 registration data for 1409 Los Angeles tracts (education, income, ownership, party affiliation), and artificial data generated to satisfy King's own model assumptions. Central finding: in every REAL dataset tested, the crude neighborhood model outperforms King's method, which is 'far from truth' and whose diagnostics fail to flag the errors (e.g. King's estimate of hispanic HS graduation is 30.1% +/- 1.1% vs truth of 55.6%, an error of 23.2 standard errors; party affiliation estimate is off by 34.6 SEs). King's method only wins on the two artificial datasets constructed to satisfy his own assumptions, and even there his own diagnostics paradoxically flag trouble. The paper also disputes King's claim of '16,000 validating comparisons', arguing this figure double-counts groups within areas and that a fair count from Table 1 alone yields thousands of counterexamples (e.g. 1133 of 1409 tracts where the neighborhood model beats King's). Conclusion: King's diagnostics are 'quite subjective' with no clear rejection criterion, and standard errors from the model do not reliably indicate actual error magnitude — a direct empirical rebuttal to the claim that EI diagnostics can validate estimates at fine geographic granularity. `evergreen`
