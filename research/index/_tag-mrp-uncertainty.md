- [[uc-davis-previously-published-works]] — UC Davis Previously Published Works — Buttice & Highton (Political Analysis, 2013) — the key empirical critique paper on MRP's real-world performance limits with conventional national survey sample sizes (N~1,500), cited by Whiteley/The Conversation as evidence that 'the conditions required for MRP to work well are not always met by practitioners.' Analyzes MRP accuracy across 89 actual US state-opinion policy items plus a controlled Monte Carlo simulation varying (a) the strength of state-level geographic covariates and (b) the population intraclass correlation (ICC, i.e., how much opinion varies between states relative to within states). Finding: MRP performance is highly variable, not uniformly good — under weak state covariates AND low population ICC, average correlation between MRP estimates and true state values is only 0.17; under strong covariates AND high ICC it reaches 0.87. Even in the best-performing condition (high covariates, high ICC), only 28% of states achieve nominal 90% confidence-interval coverage, and across all four simulated conditions more than 50% of states have coverage rates that are too high or too low — i.e., MRP's reported uncertainty intervals are frequently miscalibrated even when point estimates are decent. Identifies two necessary-but-not-sufficient conditions for MRP to work well: (1) strong geographic-level covariates that explain a substantial share of true opinion variation, and (2) substantial between-unit (interstate) heterogeneity relative to within-unit heterogeneity (high population ICC) — both of which are impossible to verify from the sample actually in hand, since the population-level ground truth needed to check them is by definition unavailable to the analyst. Explains why MRP works well for US cultural-policy issues (Lax & Phillips 2009b, Warshaw & Rodden 2012) specifically: cultural issues exhibit unusually high geographic heterogeneity that is well captured by typical MRP geographic covariates, a favorable condition that does not generalize to all issue domains. Recommends larger sample sizes (tested N=10,000 vs N=1,500) as one lever to improve reliability, alongside deliberate, question-specific selection of geographic covariates rather than reusing a single generic covariate set across many different opinion items. `evergreen`
