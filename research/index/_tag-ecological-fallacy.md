- [[ecological-inference-and-the-ecological-fallacy]] — Ecological Inference and the Ecological Fallacy — Freedman's 1999 encyclopedia entry (Technical Report 549) is the canonical pedagogical statement of the ecological fallacy and a critique of ecological-inference (EI) methods including King's (1997) random-coefficients model. Uses Robinson's (1950) 1930 US Census example: across the 48 states, the ecological correlation between percent foreign-born and percent literate is +.53, but the individual-level correlation is -.11 — the ecological correlation gives the wrong sign. Replicates the reversal with 1995 Current Population Survey data on nativity and income: ecological correlation +.52 (suggesting foreign-born have higher incomes), but truth is 35% of native-born vs 28% of foreign-born have high income (individual correlation -.05) — driven by immigrants concentrating in richer states (CA, HI, NY vs KY, TN, WV). Presents the 'method of bounds' (Duncan & Davis 1953) using Washington state 1995 data: with only marginals known (7.9% foreign-born, 34.4% high-income), the native-born high-income share is bounded between 28.8% and 37.4% — often too wide to be useful. Table 2 directly compares neighborhood model (34%/36%), ecological regression (29%/85%), and King's random-coefficients EZIDOS model (30%/72%) against ground truth (35%/28%) on the nativity-income data: the neighborhood model is dramatically closer to truth than either regression-based method, which get the foreign-born estimate off by roughly 3x (85% and 72% vs true 28%). Concludes aggregation bias and confounding 'are unlikely to be resolved in the proximate future.' `evergreen`
